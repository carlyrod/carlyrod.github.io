## Research

### Background
Aging is a leading risk factor for many neurological diseases. With increased age, brain metabolism, plasticity, and inflammation become dysregulated, leaving neurons more vulnerable to disease pathology.

Aging is a highly heterogeneous process, influenced by interactions of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. Identification of genetic components that modify neuronal resilience to aging offer vast potential for improving the prevention and treatment of age-related neurological diseases.

Pluripotent stem cell (PSC) models are a powerful tool for investigating genetic modifiers of complex biological processes and disease. This field, however, is currently limited by an incomplete understanding of how to study age in PSC-derived cells *in vitro*.

### Approach
My thesis work aims to overcome this barrier through two different approaches to studying the genetic basis of aging pathways *in vitro*. My **first aim** focuses on defining the capacity of PSC-derived neurons to exhibit aging phenotypes *in vitro*. Alternatively, my **second aim**  I am using mouse emrbyonic stem cells (mESCs) to establish these methods which can then be translated to human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) models.

### Thesis Project Aims
- **Aim 1:** Assess the capacity of mESC-derived neurons to exhibit aging phenotypes
- **Aim 2:** Evaluate the association between the genetic basis of neuronal resiliency to stress and aging

### Other on-going Projects
- **Microglial & Neuronal co-cultures:** In collaboration with other researchers and labs at JAX, I have been helping set up hiPSC-derived microglial and neuronal co-cultures to investigate how specific disease-associated genetic variants alter microglial function and neuronal health
